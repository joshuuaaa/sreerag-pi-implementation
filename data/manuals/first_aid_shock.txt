SHOCK – FIRST AID REFERENCE

OVERVIEW
Shock is a life-threatening condition where the circulatory system fails to deliver sufficient oxygen to the body's organs and tissues. Without treatment, shock progresses to organ failure and death. It is a common complication of serious injuries and medical events.

TYPES OF SHOCK

Hypovolemic Shock (most common in trauma)
Caused by significant fluid loss: blood loss (haemorrhagic shock), severe dehydration, severe burns.
Primary treatment: control bleeding, IV fluids (medical), lay flat with legs elevated.

Cardiogenic Shock
Heart pump failure: heart attack, severe heart failure, cardiac tamponade.
Primary treatment: emergency cardiac care.

Distributive Shock
Abnormal vasodilation: septic shock (severe infection), anaphylactic shock (severe allergic reaction), neurogenic shock (spinal cord injury).
Anaphylaxis requires immediate epinephrine (EpiPen).

Obstructive Shock
Physical obstruction to blood flow: tension pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism, cardiac tamponade.
Requires emergency medical intervention.

RECOGNISING SHOCK – EARLY AND LATE SIGNS

Early Signs (Compensated Shock)
- Restlessness, anxiety, feeling "something is wrong"
- Mildly rapid heart rate (>100 bpm)
- Pale skin beginning
- Mildly increased respiratory rate
- Cool, slightly clammy skin

Late Signs (Decompensated – Critical)
- Pale, grey, blue-tinged skin, lips, fingernails (cyanosis)
- Cold, clammy, sweaty skin all over body
- Rapid, weak, thready pulse (too fast to count easily)
- Rapid, shallow breathing
- Significant confusion, disorientation, or combativeness
- Extreme thirst (major sign of blood loss)
- Nausea and vomiting
- Weakness, feeling faint, or actual fainting
- Decreased responsiveness or unconsciousness
- Blood pressure dropping (difficult to assess without equipment)

MNEMONIC – SHOCK SIGNS: Pallor, Perspiration, Prostration (collapse), Pulselessness (weak), Rapid breathing

GENERAL SHOCK FIRST AID

STEP 1 – CALL 911 IMMEDIATELY
Shock is always a medical emergency. Do not delay calling.

STEP 2 – TREAT THE CAUSE IF POSSIBLE
Control external bleeding (direct pressure, tourniquet).
For anaphylaxis: administer epinephrine auto-injector if available.
Do NOT delay treating the cause.

STEP 3 – POSITION
Standard: Lay person flat on back.
Legs elevation: Raise legs 6–12 inches (30 cm) using a folded blanket, pillow, or rolled clothing. This shifts blood from lower extremities toward vital organs.
EXCEPTIONS – Do NOT elevate legs if:
- Head, neck, or spinal injury suspected
- Breathing difficulty
- Unconscious
- Burns on legs
- Fractures of legs

For unresponsive person who is breathing: recovery position (on side).
Pregnant person: tilt onto LEFT side to prevent compression of inferior vena cava.

STEP 4 – MAINTAIN BODY TEMPERATURE
Cover with blanket, coat, or any available material.
Insulate from cold ground (place something beneath person too).
Hypothermia worsens all types of shock significantly.
Move out of wind, rain, or cold environment if possible without moving injured spine.
Do NOT overheat – goal is prevent heat loss.

STEP 5 – OXYGEN (if available)
High-flow oxygen via non-rebreather mask significantly improves outcomes if oxygen is available.
First aiders generally do not carry oxygen.

STEP 6 – DO NOT GIVE FOOD OR FLUIDS
Do NOT give anything by mouth to person in shock.
Exception: anaphylaxis – follow up epinephrine with supine position and fluids supervised.
Person may complain of intense thirst – reassure but do not give drinks.
Reason: may require general anaesthesia, and aspiration is dangerous.

STEP 7 – REASSURE AND MONITOR
Speak calmly and continuously. Reassurance reduces anxiety, which reduces heart rate.
Monitor: Pulse rate and quality. Respiratory rate and quality. Skin colour and temperature. Level of consciousness.
Use AVPU scale: Alert, Voice (responds to voice), Pain (responds to pain only), Unresponsive.

ANAPHYLACTIC SHOCK (Severe Allergic Reaction) – SPECIAL MANAGEMENT

Triggers: bee stings, nuts, shellfish, medications, latex, exercise.
Onset: typically within minutes of exposure.

SIGNS OF ANAPHYLAXIS:
- Skin: hives, flushing, swelling of face/lips/tongue/throat
- Respiratory: stridor, wheezing, difficulty breathing, hoarse voice
- Cardiovascular: rapid weak pulse, dizziness, fainting
- Gastrointestinal: nausea, vomiting, cramping

ANAPHYLAXIS TREATMENT:
1. EPINEPHRINE FIRST (auto-injector/EpiPen): inject into outer mid-thigh, hold for 10 seconds.
2. Call 911 immediately even after epinephrine – biphasic reactions can occur.
3. Give second epinephrine dose after 5–15 minutes if symptoms persist.
4. Lay person flat (unless breathing difficulty – then sitting upright preferred).
5. CPR if cardiac arrest.
6. Antihistamines are NOT a substitute for epinephrine in severe anaphylaxis.

SEPTIC SHOCK
From severe infection disseminating to bloodstream.
Signs: fever or hypothermia + shock signs.
First aid: supportive. Call 911. Keep warm. No specific field treatment.

NEUROGENIC SHOCK (Spinal Cord Injury)
Caused by: loss of sympathetic tone below injury level.
Characteristics: warm, dry skin (paradoxically) despite shock state; bradycardia (slow heart rate).
Specific first aid: immobilise spine, keep warm, call 911.
Do NOT elevate legs if spinal injury suspected.

PAEDIATRIC SHOCK
Children compensate more effectively than adults but decompensate suddenly.
Signs in children: mottled skin, prolonged capillary refill >2 seconds, abnormal mental status, weak central pulse.
Normal heart rates in children are higher than adults – tachycardia threshold differs by age.
Any child with shock signs: call 911 immediately.

WHAT TO REPORT TO EMERGENCY SERVICES
When calling emergency services, provide:
1. What happened (mechanism: fall, car accident, allergic reaction, etc.)
2. Signs of shock observed
3. Any bleeding controlled
4. Medications given (epinephrine, etc.)
5. Time of injury and current symptoms
