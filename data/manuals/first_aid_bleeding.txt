BLEEDING AND HEMORRHAGE CONTROL – FIRST AID REFERENCE

OVERVIEW
Uncontrolled bleeding is one of the most preventable causes of traumatic death. Rapid, effective hemorrhage control within the first few minutes is critical to survival.

SEVERITY CLASSIFICATION

Minor Bleeding
Small cuts, abrasions, or superficial lacerations. Oozing or slow dripping. Usually stops with minimal intervention. Risk of infection is primary concern.

Moderate Bleeding
Steady flow from deeper cuts or lacerations. Requires sustained direct pressure. May take 10–20 minutes to control. Risk of significant blood loss if untreated.

Severe / Life-Threatening Bleeding
Spurting or pulsing blood indicates arterial involvement. Large volumes visible pooling. Wound that won't stop despite pressure. Requires aggressive, immediate intervention.

DIRECT PRESSURE – PRIMARY INTERVENTION

Direct pressure is the first and most important step for all external bleeding.

PROCEDURE:
1. Put on gloves if available. If not, use extra layers of cloth as a barrier.
2. Apply a clean cloth, gauze pad, or clothing directly over the wound.
3. Press FIRMLY with the heel of your hand. Use your body weight, not just arm strength.
4. Maintain CONSTANT pressure. Do not lift or peek – lifting breaks the clot.
5. If blood soaks through, add more material ON TOP of the first layer. Do NOT remove the original material.
6. Hold pressure for a minimum of 10 minutes by the clock without checking.
7. For scalp wounds: apply even more pressure – scalp bleeds heavily.

WOUND PACKING (for deep or cavity wounds)
For gunshot wounds, stab wounds, or deep gashes that cannot be compressed from outside:
1. Push clean gauze firmly into the wound cavity.
2. Pack tightly until the cavity is full.
3. Apply firm external pressure over the packed wound.
4. This method is used by military medics and is highly effective.

TOURNIQUET APPLICATION
Use when: direct pressure fails for extremity (arm or leg) bleeding, or bleeding is so severe that direct pressure is impossible while also calling for help.

PROCEDURE:
1. Position 2–3 inches above the wound (between the wound and the heart).
2. Never place over a joint.
3. If using a commercial tourniquet (CAT): wrap around limb, thread through buckle, pull tight, then wind the windlass rod until bleeding stops.
4. If improvising: use a strip of cloth at least 2 inches wide (never rope or wire). Tie a half-knot, place a stick/pen over it, tie a full knot over the stick, then twist until bleeding stops.
5. NOTE THE TIME of application. Write it on the person's forehead or arm.
6. DO NOT remove once applied. Leave for medical personnel.
7. Expect significant pain – this is normal and necessary.

ELEVATION
For extremity wounds: elevate the injured limb above heart level while maintaining pressure. Reduces blood pressure at the wound site and slows bleeding. This is a secondary measure – never delay direct pressure for elevation.

PRESSURE POINTS
If direct pressure is failing for arm bleeding: press on the brachial artery (inner upper arm) against the arm bone.
For leg bleeding: press on the femoral artery (groin crease) with the heel of both hands.

RECOGNISING SHOCK FROM BLOOD LOSS
Watch for these signs indicating significant blood loss:
- Pale, gray, or blue-tinged skin around lips and fingernails
- Cold, clammy, moist skin
- Rapid, weak, thready pulse
- Rapid shallow breathing
- Confusion, restlessness, or reduced responsiveness
- Extreme thirst

SHOCK MANAGEMENT ALONGSIDE BLEEDING CONTROL:
1. Keep the person lying flat. Do NOT prop up the head.
2. Elevate legs 6–12 inches if no spinal injury is suspected.
3. Keep warm with a blanket – hypothermia worsens bleeding.
4. Reassure calmly – anxiety increases heart rate and blood loss.
5. Do NOT give food or water – may be needed for surgery.

SCALP AND HEAD WOUNDS
Scalp is extremely vascular. Even minor head wounds bleed profusely.
Apply firm pressure. Be alert for signs of concussion: confusion, unequal pupils, vomiting, loss of consciousness.
Do NOT press on skull fractures – press around the wound instead.

INTERNAL BLEEDING
Suspected when: person shows shock signs with no visible wound, complains of abdominal or chest pain after trauma, abdomen becomes rigid or distended.
YOU CANNOT TREAT INTERNAL BLEEDING. Call emergency services immediately. Keep person still and warm while waiting.

WOUND CARE AFTER BLEEDING STOPS
1. Gently clean wound with clean running water for 5–10 minutes.
2. Remove visible debris but do NOT probe deep wounds.
3. Apply antibiotic ointment if available.
4. Cover with sterile or clean bandage.
5. Change bandage daily or when wet/dirty.
6. Watch for infection signs: redness spreading, warmth, swelling, pus, fever, red streaks leading away from wound (indicates spreading infection requiring immediate medical care).

WHEN TO SEEK EMERGENCY CARE
- Any arterial (spurting) bleed
- Bleeding that won't stop after 10 minutes of proper direct pressure
- Deep puncture wounds, bite wounds, or wounds with embedded objects
- Signs of shock
- Head injuries with loss of consciousness
- Wounds over joints, tendons, or bones
- Any wound requiring stitches (gaping, >0.5 inch, or on face)
