FRACTURES AND MUSCULOSKELETAL INJURIES – FIRST AID REFERENCE

OVERVIEW
A fracture is a break in a bone. Fractures range from hairline cracks to complete breaks with displacement. First aid goals are: prevent further injury, relieve pain, and arrange transport to definitive care.

TYPES OF FRACTURES

Closed (Simple) Fracture
Bone broken but skin intact. Most common. Standard first aid applies.

Open (Compound) Fracture
Bone end penetrates through skin, or wound extends to the bone. HIGH INFECTION RISK. Treat as both a fracture and a wound. Seek emergency care.

Greenstick Fracture
Bone bends and partially breaks (like a green stick). Common in children. Less severe but still requires medical evaluation.

Stress Fracture
Hairline crack from repetitive stress. Pain worsens with activity. Requires imaging to diagnose.

Compression Fracture
Vertebra collapses under pressure. Common with falls in older adults. Can injure spinal cord.

RECOGNISING A FRACTURE
Signs and symptoms:
- Pain: severe, localised, worse with movement or pressure
- Swelling and bruising at injury site
- Deformity: limb looks bent or out of position
- Crepitus: grinding or crunching sensation or sound (do NOT test for this)
- Loss of function: unable to use or move the limb normally
- Shortening: limb appears shorter than opposite side
- Numbness or tingling below injury (nerve or blood vessel involvement)
- Open wound with visible bone

Note: Pain and inability to bear weight reliably indicate fracture. You don't need to see the bone to suspect a fracture.

GENERAL FRACTURE FIRST AID PRINCIPLES

1. DO NOT attempt to straighten or realign the bone.
2. Immobilise the fracture in the position found.
3. Immobilise the joint ABOVE and BELOW the fracture site.
4. Support the injured limb at all times.
5. Apply ice wrapped in cloth to reduce swelling (do not apply ice directly to skin).
6. Elevate if possible without causing additional pain.
7. Check circulation, sensation, and movement (CSM) below the injury.
8. Seek medical evaluation – imaging is required to confirm and properly treat fractures.

SPLINTING
Splinting prevents further displacement and reduces pain during transport.

Materials: Commercial splint, rolled newspaper, cardboard, sturdy sticks, pillow.
Padding: Pad splint with soft material (clothing, towels) to prevent pressure sores.
Positioning: Pad and splint in position of comfort, not anatomical alignment.
Securing: Use ties (strips of cloth, bandages) above and below the fracture site, never over the fracture.
Check after splinting: Ensure circulation is not compromised. Check CSM (can they feel touch below injury, can they wiggle fingers or toes, is skin pink?).

SPECIFIC FRACTURE MANAGEMENT

ARM FRACTURES (Forearm, Wrist)
Place arm in position of comfort across chest.
Pad and splint from palm to above elbow if possible.
Apply sling using triangular bandage or improvised (shirt, scarf).
Check hand – fingers should remain visible and be checked for circulation.

UPPER ARM (Humerus) FRACTURES
Support arm in sling against body.
Bind arm gently to body with additional bandage for stability.
Do NOT attempt to splint the humerus against the body with force.

ELBOW FRACTURES
DO NOT attempt to straighten a bent elbow.
Immobilise in position found.
Check radial pulse (wrist pulse) frequently – brachial artery injury is common.

LOWER LEG FRACTURES (Tibia, Fibula)
If foot is below the fracture, carefully pad and apply a long splint along the outside of the leg.
Improvised: secure leg to a straight board, padded well.
Check pedal pulse (top of foot), sensation, and toe movement.
Do not allow weight-bearing.

FEMUR (THIGH BONE) FRACTURE – EMERGENCY
Femur fractures can cause 2–3 litres of internal blood loss.
Treat for shock immediately (lay flat, keep warm).
Call 911 – requires definitive orthopedic care.
Traction splint (if trained) reduces blood loss and pain.
Do NOT attempt field reduction.

PELVIS FRACTURES – EMERGENCY
Extremely dangerous due to proximity of major blood vessels.
Do NOT move person unless immediate life threat.
Keep person lying flat.
Call 911. Treat for shock.

SPINAL FRACTURES – CRITICAL
Suspect spinal injury with: high-speed trauma, fall from height, diving accidents, direct blow to spine, axial loading (fall landing on feet or head).
DO NOT move the person unless life is in immediate danger.
If person must be moved: log-roll technique with at least 3 people, maintaining alignment of head, neck, and spine.
Apply manual in-line stabilisation to head/neck if trained.
Assume spinal injury in all unconscious trauma victims.

OPEN (COMPOUND) FRACTURES
Do NOT push bone back in or probe the wound.
Control surrounding bleeding with gentle pressure around (not on) the wound.
Cover bone and wound with a moist sterile dressing.
Immobilise in position found.
Seek emergency care – high risk of osteomyelitis (bone infection) if not treated promptly.

JOINT DISLOCATIONS
Characteristics: Joint appears deformed, out of position; person guards it. Common joints: shoulder, finger, kneecap, hip.
DO NOT attempt to reduce (put back in place) unless trained – may trap nerves or vessels.
Immobilise in position found, apply ice, seek emergency care.
Exception: finger dislocations are sometimes reduced by lay persons after ensuring no fracture.

SPRAINS AND STRAINS
RICE protocol:
- Rest: stop activity, protect from further injury
- Ice: 20 minutes every 2–3 hours for 48–72 hours
- Compression: elastic bandage (not too tight)
- Elevation: above heart level when possible

Seek care if: unable to bear weight, severe swelling, deformity, or no improvement after 48 hours.

CHECKING CIRCULATION, SENSATION, MOVEMENT (CSM)
Always check below the injury:
- Circulation: capillary refill under 2 seconds, skin pink and warm, palpable distal pulse
- Sensation: ask patient if they can feel touch below injury
- Movement: ask to wiggle fingers or toes
Recheck after splinting. Compromised circulation is a surgical emergency.
